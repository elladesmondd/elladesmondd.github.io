Why digital accessibility? 

The United States has had a long and convoluted history with accessibility law. From the Smith-Fess Act in 1920 (the first federally funded vocational rehabilitation program for physically disabled individuals) to the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (which prohibited discrimination based on disability for programs receiving federal funding) all the way to the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) in 1990. ADA Title II and III now either strictly (or generally) mandate that federal and public facing websites comply with digital accessibility standards.

Accessibility has always been something people had to fight for, rather than something seen as standard or commonplace. People with disabilities account for the U.S.’s largest minority group, and are the only minority group that anyone can join at any time (Disability Funders). So, if you’re not practicing accessibility for others, learn it for your future self, who could be afflicted by the same issues which 54 million Americans currently face.

While much of our physical infrastructure was built with accessibility as an afterthought, our digital infrastructure doesn’t have to be that way. As professional writers, we have the choice to think about everyone in our designs.

What is accessibility?

We hear it all the time, but what does accessibility truly mean? Accessibility is defined as “the quality of being able to be reached or entered” (Oxford English Dictionary). This is, ultimately, vague. This definition does not delegate the ease in which it can be reached or entered, it does not delegate by whom it can be reached or entered. A building with a meters-long zig zag ramp is technically accessible, but it takes five times as long to enter than those using the stairs. When we think of accessibility, we need to consider more than disabled people simply being able to achieve the same result as those without disabilities - we need to think of how easy it is for them to do.

The U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights defines accessibility like so: “a person with a disability is afforded the opportunity to acquire the same information, engage in the same interactions, and enjoy the same services as a person without a disability in an equally effective and equally integrated manner, with substantially equivalent ease of use.”

To simplify it, accessibility can be thought of as a “unilateral availability to all,” regardless of physical or mental capabilities.

What is disability?

The ADA defines a person with a disability as “a person who has a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activity,” though they make it clear that this is a legal definition and not a medical one. There are three main categories which disabilities fall under (May 10-13):

  • Sensory: Affect the ability to see, hear, or feel

  • Physical: Affect mobility, dexterity, or stamina

  • Intellectual, developmental, neurological, and psychological: Affect the brain or nervous system

Truthfully, there is no clear-cut, all encompassing definition for disability. They take on too many shapes and forms, affect too many parts of life to live under the same umbrella. So, our choice as professional writers is this: do we simply cover our bases, or do we structure our designs to hopefully give every person a unilateral access to our products?

Universal Design

Have you ever lugged a heavy suitcase through a crowded city and praised the curb ramps, or the elevators in and out of train stations? Have you ever opened a door with your hands full and appreciated the ease of a lever door handle? Have you ever relished in the large font size on a website that didn’t strain your eyes? These are all examples of “universal design.”

The Centre for Excellence in Universal Design defines the concept as: “the design and composition of an environment so that it can be accessed, understood and used to the greatest extent possible by all people regardless of their age, size, ability or disability.” They say it’s a fundamental aspect of good design.

When thinking about accessibility, it’s easy to get caught in the blackhole of making sure you checked every box, for every possible impairment that could possibly exist. Universal design attempts to quell this stress. Make your thing good. Make it a pleasure to use for everyone. Keep those with disabilities in mind, and you make something that’s easy for everyone to interact with.

In digital accessibility, this looks like closed captions (good for deaf people, and people trying to watch a video in a loud environment), alt text (good for people using screenreaders, and people with spotty service), voice-activated tech (good for people with mobility issues, or for when you’d just rather tell the computer what to do) and so much more.
